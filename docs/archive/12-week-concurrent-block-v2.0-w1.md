# 12-Week Concurrent Training Block — v2.0-w1

**Athlete:** M38, 6'0", 170 lb · **Built against:** Astra Coaching Prompt v2.0 · **Mode:** A (build the block)
**Strength days:** Sunday PM, Monday PM, Wednesday PM, Friday AM · **Rest:** Saturday

---

## 1. Executive findings

The block resolves to four fixed session identities, one lower-body strength exposure on Monday and one on Friday, and a single high-quality elastic-and-acceleration slot on Friday ahead of the squat. Every priority lift owns a fresh slot on a day where nothing else competes for it: weighted dip on Sunday, conventional deadlift on Monday, strict overhead press on Wednesday, low-bar squat on Friday. The overhead press gets the freshest upper-body day of the week — Wednesday sits 48 hours after Monday's session and roughly 34 hours after Tuesday's ride — because it is the block's first strength priority and the only lift whose 12-week target requires the loading to be near-perfect.

Sunday carries the pressing volume and the paused bench because it is the one day preceded by a hard ride. Cycling interferes with subsequent resistance work far less than running does, and what interference exists lands on the legs, so the ride-compromised day is given upper-body pressing and no lower-body strength work and no elastic work at all.

The overhead press progression is built on a Wednesday top set that moves 110 × 3 in week 1 to 125 × 2 in week 11, with four 2.5 lb increments and one rep-scheme step at the accumulation-to-intensification boundary. That arithmetic lands the week-12 test of 125 × 2 at 2 RIR with one increment of headroom to absorb a stall. Dips, pull-ups, and bench each run the same shape: five scheduled increments, a deload reset, and a week-11 dress rehearsal at the test load.

Elastic work is the highest-risk domain and receives the most conservative treatment in the block. Tier 3 enters in week 5 at 20 contacts — the bottom of the permitted 20–40 band — and reaches only 26 by week 11. Extended bounding is deliberately left out of this block; the elastic success criterion is a standing broad jump, and the return on adding the highest-risk bounding variant to a sprint-deconditioned athlete in the final three weeks before a test does not justify the adductor and hamstring exposure. Tier 3 progresses one stress variable at a time: volume in week 8, drop height in week 9, volume in week 10, and broad-jump test rehearsal in week 11.

Three passages in the source document conflict with each other. The deadlift exposure count, the post-deload plyometric restoration, and the week-1 broad-jump baseline each produce an impossible or self-contradictory requirement. All three are named, quoted, and resolved in section 2 below, and the resolutions are carried into the verification pass in section 21.

The squat ends the block at 240 × 2 rather than the tracked 245 × 2. That is a deliberate application of the conflict hierarchy: Friday carries the sprint session, the tier-3 jumps, and the squat, and when squat load and elastic quality compete, elastic quality ranks higher.

### Conflicts inside the source document

Three passages cannot all be satisfied as written. Each is quoted exactly, with the resolution and the smallest change that removes the conflict.

**Conflict 1 — deadlift exposure count.** §5.7 states: *"One heavy exposure every 7 days on a fixed weekday, 13 exposures across the block."* A 12-week block at one exposure per 7 days contains 12 exposures. The two halves of the sentence disagree by one. The block is built to the rule that governs training — one exposure every 7 days on a fixed weekday, Monday, for 12 exposures. Smallest change: amend §5.7 to read 12 exposures, or state that the 13th is the anchoring exposure in the week preceding week 1.

**Conflict 2 — post-deload plyometric restoration.** §5.13 requires week 6 to run *"low-tier plyo only"* at roughly half volume, and §5.8 requires *"Weeks 6 and 12 run low tier only at roughly half volume."* §12 check 10 requires that *"no tier rises more than 15% week over week."* Halving tier 1 in week 6 and then returning to the previously tolerated volume in week 7 is a 90% rise under a literal reading, and a 15%-capped week 7 would put tier 1 at 57 contacts and make the intensification phase unreachable. The cap is applied to increases above the highest previously tolerated weekly volume in that tier, and week 7 is set at or below every pre-deload figure — tier 1 at 95 against a tolerated 100, tier 2 at 29 against 29, tier 3 at 20 against 20 — so no tier exceeds a level the athlete has already handled. Check 10 is printed under both readings in section 21. Smallest change: add to §12 check 10 the clause "measured against the highest previously tolerated weekly volume in that tier."

**Conflict 3 — week-1 broad-jump baseline against the tier-3 gate.** §5.13 requires week 1 to include a *"broad-jump baseline."* §5.8 classifies *"max broad jumps"* as tier 3 and sets tier 3 at *"0 until week 5."* A maximal standing broad jump in week 1 is therefore both required and prohibited. The baseline is treated as a measurement rather than training volume: three build-up jumps at rising effort followed by three measured attempts with full recovery, taken on Friday of week 1 after the full tier-1 ramp, excluded from the tier-3 ledger, and not repeated until week 11. The same treatment applies to the week-12 test. Smallest change: add to §5.8 the clause "test measurements are excluded from tier accounting."

## 3. Evidence and practitioner review

### Controlled research

**Frequency and volume.** The volume-equated frequency meta-analyses converge on the same answer: once weekly volume is held constant, spreading it across more sessions adds little to maximal strength. Grgic and colleagues in Sports Medicine and Ralston and colleagues in Sports Medicine – Open both report no significant frequency effect under volume-equated conditions, and the recent dose-response meta-regression finds that strength gains rise with volume but with pronounced diminishing returns, and that moving from two to four weekly sessions adds nothing once volume is matched. The practical consequence for this block is that overhead press frequency is a scheduling variable rather than a stimulus variable. Three weekly exposures are used in accumulation because the third costs twelve minutes and buys skill repetitions on a lift with a defined technical sticking region, and two are used in intensification because the source document calls for press volume to trim as intensity rises. Neither choice is defended as producing more strength than the other.

**Rest intervals.** The Bayesian meta-analysis on inter-set rest finds a modest hypertrophy benefit above 60 seconds that flattens past 90, while the strength literature consistently favours longer intervals, with the older strength-focused review placing the useful range at or above two minutes for maximal-strength outcomes. Derived from that evidence alone, priority compounds in this block would sit at three to four minutes and accessories at 60 to 90 seconds. Reconciled against the 75-minute cap, that is exactly what the session cards prescribe, because the reconciliation was achieved by supersetting rather than by shortening rest. The levers used are stated in section 5.

**Concurrent interference.** Wilson and colleagues' 2012 meta-analysis is the single most applicable finding in the block: resistance training concurrent with running produced significant decrements in hypertrophy and strength, while concurrent cycling did not, with muscle-damaging eccentric work in running the proposed reason. The same analysis reports negative relationships between endurance frequency and duration and strength and power outcomes, and a power effect size for concurrent training that falls between strength-only and endurance-only training. The intra-session sequence meta-analysis supports resistance before endurance for lower-body dynamic strength over programmes of five weeks or more, and the sequencing review notes that AMPK elevation after high-intensity endurance work takes at least three hours to return to baseline, which makes the locked six-hour Sunday separation comfortably sufficient. The same-day order trial in moderately active men found order did not mediate strength, hypertrophy, or aerobic outcomes over nine weeks, though force and power were more sensitive than strength.

Two consequences follow. Cycling is the correct aerobic modality to pair with this block's strength targets, and the interference risk in this athlete comes from the running reintroduction rather than from the bike. The running ladder is therefore gated harder than the cycling is.

**Minimum effective dose and maintenance.** The powerlifter minimum-effective-dose work places the floor for maintaining and modestly improving one-rep-max strength at a handful of working sets of one to five repetitions per lift per week at RPE 7.5 to 9.5, and the lower-volume team-sport meta-analysis finds comparable adaptations from reduced volume. This is why the deadlift holds at one exposure per week with four to six work reps and no required load progression, and why the squat runs three work sets. Maintaining an existing capacity is cheap; that is the basis of the source document's decision to rank deadlift maintenance above squat and bench improvement.

**RIR reliability.** The intraset RIR prediction work in resistance-trained lifters reports good raw and absolute accuracy at 1-RIR and 3-RIR on the bench press, and the reliability work reports tighter coefficients of variation at three and five repetitions than at eight. Experienced lifters under-predict repetitions to failure by roughly one to two, while less experienced lifters under-predict by four to five. This supports the source document's caution that RIR at two repetitions under heavy load systematically underestimates reserve, and it supports the dual-anchor rule: prescribe from the low-rep e1RM, cross-check against target RIR, and correct the load the following session when they disagree.

**Plyometric dosing.** The ground-contact-frequency meta-analysis in adolescents found fewer than 900 total contacts optimal for countermovement jump and more than 1400 for squat jump, and the trained-adult meta-analysis found contact volume a significant moderator of countermovement jump and 20 m sprint, with the largest effects above 1400 touches. Practitioner dosing sits at 50 to 200 contacts per session by intensity, with the strongest adaptation-per-risk in the 50 to 100 range. This block totals roughly 1,400 contacts across twelve weeks, which places it in the productive range while keeping every individual session between 45 and 95 contacts. The athlete's current exposure is minimal, so the block is weighted toward the low end of intensity and the high end of caution rather than toward the contact totals that maximise group means.

**Tendon.** The systematic review on tendon adaptation to mechanical loading finds that stiffness adaptation depends primarily on loading magnitude, that higher-strain protocols outperform low-strain protocols, and that interventions of twelve weeks or more produce larger effects, with loading frequency and rate far less determinative. Twelve weeks is the minimum useful duration for connective-tissue change, which is precisely the block length. The heavy low-rep pressing and pulling work supplies the high-strain stimulus; the plyometric ladder supplies the rate stimulus. Neither is expected to produce measurable tendon change inside this block, and none is claimed.

**Acceleration and hamstring exposure.** The uphill and downhill running review and the sprint-biomechanics work on hamstring strain support the source document's ordering: incline running reduces peak velocity, shortens stride, and lowers the eccentric demand on the hamstring during late swing, which is the phase where strain injuries concentrate. The high-speed-running load literature finds that consistent exposure without rapid short-term increases is protective, and that large weekly jumps in high-speed and sprint distance raise injury risk. The ladder in section 13 rises by one variable per week and never jumps more than one step.

**Adductor prevention.** The Adductor Strengthening Programme cluster-randomised trial reports roughly 41% lower risk of self-reported groin problems across a season, and the systematic review on the Copenhagen adduction exercise reports consistent improvement in eccentric hip adduction strength, hip range of motion, and symptom scores. The low-dose randomised trial shows that a small weekly dose produces strength change. This is the strongest prevention evidence in the block and it is applied at the mandated twice-weekly frequency with a short-lever start.

**Periodisation, cluster sets, deloads, and age.** The volume-equated periodisation meta-analysis finds periodised training superior to non-periodised for one-rep-max strength, with undulating models outperforming linear models in trained but not untrained lifters. The cluster-set meta-analyses find equivalent strength outcomes to traditional sets with better within-session velocity maintenance and lower perceived effort, which is why clusters are the second reconciliation lever rather than a default. The deloading survey and coaches' practice work report deloads of roughly 6.4 days integrated every 5.6 weeks, which matches the locked weeks 6 and 12 almost exactly; one controlled trial found a mid-programme deload week slightly reduced lower-body strength outcomes without affecting hypertrophy, power, or endurance, so the deloads here are prescribed for fatigue dissipation rather than for performance gain. The masters and rate-of-force-development literature reports that power declines faster than strength with age and that lifelong strength-trained older athletes retain type II fibre distribution and rate of force development comparable to young athletes, with explosive and heavy-resistance training both improving rapid force production. At 38, this athlete is on the flat part of that curve; the relevance is that the elastic work is a use-it-or-lose-it quality and earns its place in the block.

**Jump orientation.** The meta-analytical comparison of vertically and horizontally oriented plyometric training does not confirm the force-vector prediction that orientation-matched training transfers better, and parallel-design trials show both orientations improving sprint and change-of-direction outcomes. Standing broad jump training does improve standing broad jump over eight weeks. The block therefore uses vertical, bilateral, low-amplitude work to build tissue tolerance first and adds horizontal work later, with a broad-jump rehearsal in week 11 for test familiarity rather than for a specificity effect.

### Practitioner material, labelled separately from controlled research

Joel Smith's Just Fly Sports work and his Elastic Essentials material treat bounding as the central expression of elastic capacity and emphasise reflexive and connective-tissue qualities over contact totals. His plyometric-progression framework and the SimpliFaster sprint-dosage material both argue for quality-gated volume rather than fixed prescriptions, with the session ending when contact quality degrades rather than when a rep count is reached. The Sportsmith hill-sprint article supports the incline-first reintroduction on the same mechanical grounds as the controlled literature. This block adopts the quality gate — every elastic and sprint block in section 12 and section 13 carries a stop-on-quality-loss rule — and declines the bounding emphasis for this athlete in this block, for the reason given in section 1.

### Where evidence and this document disagree

The source document requires 16 to 20 compound pressing work sets per week. The dose-response meta-regression shows strength gains flattening well before that volume, so a smaller pressing dose would likely produce a similar overhead press outcome at lower recovery cost. The document's floor is followed, because §2 places this document above retrieved evidence and because the floor is defensible for a lifter whose three top priorities are all pressing or pressing-adjacent. The disagreement is recorded here rather than resolved silently, and the pressing volume sits at 19 sets in accumulation and 18 in intensification, toward the lower half of the permitted band.

The dip progression is therefore the one primary lift where the weekly audit carries more weight than the plan, and the week-2 and week-4 increments are the first to be held if bar speed or elbow response says so.

## 5. Training architecture and weekly stress organisation

The week is organised around one question: which day can afford to lose quality. Sunday is preceded by a long ride, so it loses lower-body quality and keeps upper-body quality; it gets the pressing volume and the paused bench and no elastic work. Monday is roughly 20 hours after Sunday's session and 30 hours after the long ride, so it loses some systemic freshness; it gets the deadlift, which is a maintenance exposure of four to six heavy reps and does not need a peak day, and the loaded pull-up, which is unaffected by prior cycling. Wednesday is the freshest upper-body day of the week and gets the overhead press. Friday is the freshest lower-body day and gets the elastic work, the sprint work, and the squat, in that order.

Elastic and running sit outside the 75-minute strength cap but are counted in every fatigue decision. Their footprint is 6 minutes on Monday, 14 minutes on Wednesday, and 20 to 50 minutes on Friday depending on the week. The squat follows Friday's elastic block because §5.8 requires elastic work to precede lower-body lifting whenever they share a session, and because a sprint session on fatigued legs is the single most likely way to produce the adductor injury this block is built to avoid.

The two lower-body strength exposures are Monday and Friday, four days apart in one direction and three in the other. Unilateral and knee-dominant secondary work gets no slot of its own; Copenhagen adduction on Monday and Friday supplies the unilateral lower-body exposure and is a hard-floor item in its own right. There is no belt squat, leg press, or step-up in the block. The lower-body dose is not two sessions — it is two strength sessions plus three to four rides plus 45 to 173 plyometric contacts plus 45 to 230 metres of sprinting, and the strength exposures are the smallest part of that.

Rest periods were derived from the evidence first and reconciled against the budget second. The reconciliation levers used, in the document's order of preference, are: **antagonist supersetting during rest on non-priority work**, applied to every accessory block and to Friday's dip-and-pull-up pairing; and **cluster sets on heavy low-rep work**, held in reserve as the week-8-onward option for the fourth Monday pull-up set and the fourth Sunday dip set. No set was removed and no rest period on a priority compound was shortened. Priority compounds run three to four minutes, secondary compounds two and a half to three, and supersetted accessories 60 to 90 seconds.

The accumulation phase runs a three-rep top set on the overhead press with three back-off sets at 92.5%. The intensification phase drops to a two-rep top set with matched back-offs, which is the single planned rep-scheme change in the block and the reason the press volume trims from 19 to 18 weekly sets without losing progression.

## 9. OHP calibration and progression plan

The calibration single sits inside week 1's normal Wednesday session, in the fresh slot, after the standard ramp. Ramp 45 × 6, 75 × 4, 95 × 3, 105 × 2, 112.5 × 1, then continue in 5 to 7.5 lb steps taking singles until the first rep that is strict but no longer crisp. That rep is the calibration single. Stop there. The three prescribed work sets at 105 follow regardless of the result, because the point of week 1 is a training week that happens to reveal a number.

The technical standard governs whether the single counts. No knee dip, no hip drive, no rebound out of the rack position, no push-press initiation, knees locked throughout, a controlled settled start rather than an immediate drive, and a repeatable finish position. A single that violates any of these is not a calibration result and the week's prescription stands unchanged.

Expect 117.5 to 122.5 from the stated provisional e1RM of 128 to 130. That range requires no action.

**If the single lands materially above the provisional e1RM.** A single at 145 implies an e1RM near 158, which is roughly 22% above the provisional figure. The response is not a 22% rescale on one observation. First confirm the technical standard was met and that the rack position was settled. Then apply a capped rescale: raise every OHP prescription by no more than 10%, hold there through week 2, and re-examine against the week-2 top set's RIR and bar-speed grade. If week 2 confirms, apply the remainder of the rescale in week 3 and raise the week-12 target by the same total proportion. The rescale and the revised week-12 target both require approval before they take effect, per §10 change authority. The reason for capping is in §5.4: RIR accuracy is poor at two reps under heavy load, and one RPE-graded single is weaker evidence than six weeks of set data pointing the other way.

**If the single lands below 112.5.** Treat it as a fatigue artefact rather than a rescale trigger, keep week 1 as written, and grade the week-2 top set before drawing any conclusion. A downward rescale requires two comparable exposures, not one.

In estimated-one-rep-max terms the block runs from roughly 127 to roughly 139, which is the +10 lb the week-12 target asks for. The week-11 rehearsal at the exact test load means the test itself is a repeat rather than a new maximum. One stall is affordable: skipping any single increment still lands 125 × 2 at 2 RIR in week 12, because week 11 would then arrive at 122.5 × 2 and the test load remains one increment away.

**Never catch up a missed increment.** A held week is a held week; the next increment arrives on schedule at the next scheduled point, not sooner and not larger.

### The sticking region and the pin press

The limitation sits between chest and forehead, which is a mid-range force problem rather than a lockout problem, so no triceps-biased overload appears anywhere in this block for the purpose of solving it. The permitted tool is a pin press from the demonstrated sticking region, and it is not scheduled. It becomes available only on repeated evidence, defined as: the Wednesday top set fails or turns grindy at the same height on two consecutive comparable exposures, with technique clean and the adductor and fatigue domains green. The trial then replaces the Monday technical exposure — not any full-range work — with a pin press at the measured sticking height for 3 × 2–3 at 4 RIR, for two weeks. Full-range OHP stays central throughout. At most one such variation exists in the block, and it requires approval as a primary-lift change.

## 10. Dip, pull-up, and bench strategies

**Weighted dip.** The dip is the block's second priority and gets the fresh slot on Sunday, before any pressing fatigue exists. It is a pressing exposure for both volume and fatigue accounting, which is why Sunday is a pressing-priority session and why no other heavy press shares that slot. The standard is a consistent deep range below 90 degrees at the elbow with a controlled lockout, and both external load and total system load are tracked — at 170 lb bodyweight, the week-12 test of +50 × 6 is 220 lb of total system load for six repetitions. The heavy outcome each week is a clean set with reserve rather than a maximum attempt. The second exposure on Friday runs 20 lb lighter at 3 RIR and is antagonist-supersetted with pull-ups, which is where the Friday time budget is recovered.

The dip is the one primary lift in this block whose programming rests on unverified evidence, as recorded in section 4. Its increments are therefore the first to be held on ambiguous information, and the weekly audit outranks the written progression for this lift specifically.

**Weighted neutral-grip pull-up.** Four standardisation points are fixed for the block and do not change: parallel neutral handles at shoulder width, a full dead hang with elbows extended at the bottom, collarbone level with the bar at the top, and the dip belt worn at the same position with the load hanging free. Any change to these invalidates comparison across weeks.

Three exposures per week: the heavy one on Monday, a volume exposure on Wednesday 15 lb lighter at 3 RIR, and two supersetted sets on Friday at +10 to +15. Late-set decline is treated as fatigue and never as tendon intolerance. The escalation path, in order, is: extend Monday's rest from three to three and a half minutes; convert Monday's fourth work set to a cluster of 2 + 2 + 1 with 20 seconds of intra-set rest; reduce Wednesday's volume sets from four to three; reduce the Monday back-off load by 5 lb. Frequency is the last thing touched, and only after all four of those have been tried.

**Paused bench.** One exposure per week, three work sets, on the ride-compromised day. It progresses 5 lb every two weeks and every increment is gated: if the preceding week's OHP top set or dip top set was graded grindy or was missed, the bench increment is skipped and the load holds. Secondary bench volume and direct triceps work are reduced before any higher-priority pressing is touched — the bench sets are labelled cut-second and the direct arm work cut-first, which is exactly that ordering expressed on the card. The pause is a full stop with the bar motionless on the chest and the setup is identical week to week.

## 11. Low-bar squat and conventional deadlift strategy

**Low-bar squat.** Low-bar is the required primary squat and it holds the Friday fresh slot, after the elastic and sprint block. Depth is at or below parallel on every work rep, and depth consistency matters more than load across this block. Long femurs with a shorter torso tilt the low-bar squat further toward the posterior chain, which means two things for the design. The squat and the deadlift are placed four days apart rather than adjacent, and the squat's weekly dose is held at three work sets — nine reps in accumulation, six in intensification — which is the smallest dose consistent with maintaining and modestly improving the lift.

The squat ends the block at 240 × 2 rather than the tracked 245 × 2. Friday carries the sprint session, the tier-3 jumps, and the squat, and when those compete the conflict hierarchy puts elastic and acceleration quality above squat improvement. Adding a fourth 5 lb step would require either an earlier increment cadence than §5.6 allows or a compromise on Friday's elastic quality. If the week-9 and week-10 squat sets are crisp and the adductor and elastic domains are green, a 245 × 3 × 2 in week 11 is available as an approved change rather than as a planned prescription.

No knee-dominant secondary lift appears in the block. There are exactly two lower-body strength exposures and there is no third slot to put one in; the time it would consume belongs to the elastic work, which outranks it.

**Conventional deadlift.** One heavy exposure every seven days, on Monday, for twelve exposures across the block. The prescription is crisp singles or doubles at RPE 7 to 8, four to six work repetitions, never intentionally ground. The deadlift is not a target in this block and carries no required load progression; it undulates between 420 and 455 to hold a clean 450 × 2 and does not build toward anything. There is no end-block maximum test.

Grip is hook or alternating mixed, with the forward hand swapped set to set, since no straps are permitted. If grip fails before the hips or back, the response is to drop the top set to singles rather than to reduce the load, because the point of the exposure is the heavy position.

Monday sits roughly 30 hours after the Sunday long ride, which is the one structural compromise in the deadlift's placement. The reversal, if the Monday pull is graded grindy on two consecutive weeks with normal technique, is to hold the deadlift load flat for the remainder of the block and to recommend that the Sunday long ride be made easier. The weekday does not move — a fixed weekday at seven-day spacing is a locked requirement.

Kettlebell swings and other hinge variants are permitted as supplements but appear nowhere in this block, because the two lower-body exposures are already accounted for and a supplemental hinge would displace elastic work. They may never replace the specific heavy conventional pull.

Ball or foam roller between the knees in supine hook-lying, progressive build to maximal effort over the first three seconds, hold, release slowly.

Both items count toward the unilateral exposure floor and both are labelled never-cut.

### Reactive gate

After every elastic or running session and again the following morning, run a single normal-or-abnormal check. Normal means no new sensation in the adductor region, groin, or proximal medial thigh beyond familiar post-training soreness. That is the entire check when the answer is normal.

When the answer is abnormal, capture seven items: location, severity out of ten, onset — during which rep or which movement, duration, effect on stride and gait, effect on squatting, and response to gentle resisted adduction.

| Finding | Response |
|---|---|
| Normal, both checks | Proceed. Two consecutive tolerated exposures with normal next-day response permit the next scheduled progression. |
| Mild familiar soreness, resolves inside 24–48 h, no movement change | Hold. Repeat the same prescription next week without progressing. This is neither grounds to progress nor grounds to diagnose. |
| Symptoms increase week over week, persist beyond 48 h, reduce output, or affect stride | Regress one step in the running ladder and remove tier-3 work. Hold Copenhagen at the current lever. Re-test the gate the following week. |
| Acute sharp pain, bruising, weakness, progressive symptoms, or movement-altering discomfort | Suspend all impact work — running, tiers 2 and 3 — and obtain clinical evaluation before resuming. Strength work in unaffected domains continues. |

Aggressive static stretching of the adductors immediately before faster running is not used at any point in this block.

## 16. Cycling placement notes

TrainerRoad programs every ride. Nothing in this block prescribes interval structure, duration, power targets, or ride content, and FTP is not a target. The permitted recommendations are limited to moving a ride to a different day, recommending a ride be made easy, and recommending a ride be skipped.

**Sunday.** The long ride runs in the morning with at least six hours before the strength session. The molecular basis for the separation is that AMPK elevation after hard endurance work takes at least three hours to return toward baseline; six hours is comfortably past that. Sunday's strength session is built to survive a compromised day — it contains no lower-body strength work and no elastic work, and its priority lift is a dip, which prior cycling barely touches.

If the Sunday ride overruns and the separation falls below six hours, the session still runs. Apply the in-session cut order from section 20 to whatever time remains and protect the fresh-slot lift first. A dip session reduced to two heavy sets is a completed session; a dip session moved to Monday is a schedule failure that compounds into the deadlift day.

**Friday's optional fourth ride.** If taken, it goes after the strength session with at least four hours of separation, and the recommendation is that it be an easy one. It is the first item removed from the week whenever Friday's sprint quality or squat bar speed declines, because it sits below both in the conflict hierarchy. If it is taken every week, Friday becomes a second two-a-day and the accumulated Friday load is the most likely source of a week-9 or week-10 decline; the watchlist in section 17 tracks that pattern explicitly.

**Thursday.** The recovery ride is the lever with the best ratio of recovery gained to training lost. When any domain shows Level 1 fatigue, the first cycling recommendation is that Thursday be ridden easy.

**If a prescribed ride is skipped.** Nothing in the strength plan changes. A skipped ride reduces total stress; it does not create room to add strength volume, and the week's prescriptions stand as written. Record it so that the weekly audit can separate a skipped ride from a recovery effect.

**If the TrainerRoad block is systematically heavier than this plan assumed.** The sequence of recommendations, in order, is: make Thursday's recovery ride easy; drop the optional Friday ride; move Tuesday's ride to a day that creates more separation from Wednesday; recommend that the Sunday long ride be shortened. Only after all four have been tried does the strength plan absorb the difference, and it absorbs it by holding progressions rather than by cutting priority work. Ride content is never modified at any step.

## 17. Autoregulation, fatigue, deload, and substitution rules

### The four fatigue levels

**Level 0 — normal.** Progress or hold as written.

**Level 1 — small adjustment.** Hold load, remove one low-priority set, extend rest, or delay one progression. The OHP technical exposure on Monday is cut first, every time. This level is one session's response and does not carry into the next week.

**Level 2 — affected-domain week.** Reduce work sets in the affected domain by 25 to 35% for the week and leave every other domain at full prescription. Technique stays familiar; no new exercises enter during a Level 2 week. An affected lower-body domain reduces squat and deadlift sets and the tier-2 and tier-3 plyometric volume; it does not reduce pressing.

**Level 3 — true deload.** Five to seven days at roughly half normal sets, 10 to 15% lighter as needed, at 4 RIR or easier. Faster running and tier-3 plyometrics are removed entirely. The cycling recommendation is that rides be made easy or skipped, without modifying their content.

One poor workout is not a deload. Scheduled deloads are weeks 6 and 12, and an unscheduled Level 3 does not cancel either of them unless it falls within ten days of one.

### Trigger and response

| Trigger | Level | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Sunday long ride runs 30 min over and compresses the separation | 0 | Session runs. Apply the section 20 cut order to the time available; protect the fresh slot. If the priority session that week is an OHP session, the OHP work sets are the last thing reduced and the accessory block is dropped whole. |
| Mild next-day adductor tightness, no gait change | 0–1 | Hold the running ladder and tier 2–3 at the current week's prescription. Repeat rather than progress. Not a diagnosis and not grounds to remove work. |
| Adductor symptoms worsen or alter stride | 2–3 | Suspend running and tiers 2 and 3. Clinical evaluation for movement-altering discomfort. Strength work in unaffected domains continues at full prescription. |

### Progression and regression defaults

Progress after comparable successful exposures at target-or-easier RIR with normal technique and bar speed, no symptoms, and no adverse trend. Hold after an isolated slow day, a minor RIR overshoot, an unusual workload week, or incomplete information. Reduce after missed work, RIR roughly two worse than target, meaningful technique or range-of-motion deterioration, repeated decline, or relevant symptoms. Restructure only for persistent late-set failure, a plateau, a recovery mismatch, an intolerance, or a planned phase transition. Change one primary variable at a time, and never catch up a missed increment.

Substitute only to solve an identified problem. Novelty is not a reason.
